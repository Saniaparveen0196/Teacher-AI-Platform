Chapter 8
The French Revolution

The French Revolution, which began in 1789, was one of the most significant events in modern history. It marked the collapse of centuries of absolute monarchy in France and introduced ideas of liberty, equality, and citizenship that would influence political movements across the world for the next two centuries.

8.1 Causes of the Revolution

By the late eighteenth century, France was divided into three estates. The First Estate consisted of the clergy, the Second Estate consisted of the nobility, and the Third Estate consisted of everyone else, including peasants, merchants, and the growing middle class known as the bourgeoisie. Although the Third Estate made up the vast majority of the population, it bore the heaviest burden of taxation while the First and Second Estates enjoyed numerous privileges and exemptions.

Several factors combined to create the conditions for revolution. France's involvement in costly wars, including support for the American Revolution, had left the royal treasury heavily in debt. King Louis XVI attempted to address the financial crisis by proposing tax reforms, but these were resisted by the privileged estates. At the same time, poor harvests in the 1780s led to food shortages and rising bread prices, causing widespread hardship among ordinary people.

The ideas of the Enlightenment also played a crucial role. Philosophers such as Voltaire, Rousseau, and Montesquieu had written extensively about individual liberty, the social contract, and the separation of powers. These ideas circulated widely among the educated middle class and gradually shaped public opinion against the absolute authority of the monarchy.

8.2 The Estates-General and the National Assembly

Facing financial collapse, Louis XVI convened the Estates-General in May 1789, an assembly representing all three estates, for the first time since 1614. Disputes quickly arose over voting procedures. Traditionally, each estate had one vote, meaning the First and Second Estates could always outvote the Third Estate combined. The representatives of the Third Estate demanded voting by head count instead, which would give them a real chance to influence outcomes given their larger number of representatives.

When this demand was rejected, the Third Estate broke away and declared itself the National Assembly, claiming to represent the true will of the French people. They took the famous Tennis Court Oath, vowing not to disband until a new constitution had been established. This act was a direct challenge to royal authority and is often seen as the symbolic beginning of the Revolution.

8.3 The Storming of the Bastille

Tensions in Paris escalated rapidly. On 14 July 1789, a crowd of Parisians stormed the Bastille, a fortress and prison that had come to symbolize royal tyranny. Although the Bastille held few prisoners at the time, its capture was highly symbolic, representing the collapse of royal authority in the capital. The date is still celebrated in France as a national holiday.

In the countryside, peasant uprisings known as the Great Fear spread rapidly, as rumors of aristocratic plots led to attacks on manor houses and the destruction of feudal documents. In response, the National Assembly abolished feudal privileges in August 1789 and adopted the Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen, which proclaimed liberty, equality, and popular sovereignty as fundamental principles.

8.4 The Radicalization of the Revolution

Over the following years, the Revolution grew increasingly radical. The monarchy was abolished in 1792, and France was declared a republic. King Louis XVI was tried for treason and executed by guillotine in January 1793, an event that shocked royal courts across Europe.

The period known as the Reign of Terror, led by Maximilien Robespierre and the Committee of Public Safety, saw thousands of people executed as enemies of the Revolution. While originally intended to defend the Republic against internal and external threats, the Terror came to be seen by many historians as a betrayal of the Revolution's own ideals of liberty and justice.

8.5 The Legacy of the Revolution

The French Revolution formally ended with the rise of Napoleon Bonaparte, who seized power in 1799. However, its ideas endured far beyond this period. The principles of liberty, equality, and fraternity became touchstones for later democratic and nationalist movements throughout the nineteenth and twentieth centuries.

Historians continue to debate the Revolution's legacy. Some emphasize its role in ending feudal privilege and establishing the modern concept of citizenship. Others point to the violence of the Terror as a caution about the dangers of radical political change. Both perspectives remain part of ongoing historical interpretation, and there is no single, universally accepted verdict on whether the Revolution's achievements outweighed its costs.

Summary:
- The French Revolution began in 1789 amid financial crisis, social inequality, and Enlightenment ideas.
- The Third Estate formed the National Assembly and took the Tennis Court Oath.
- The storming of the Bastille on 14 July 1789 became a symbol of the Revolution.
- The monarchy was abolished in 1792, and Louis XVI was executed in 1793.
- The Reign of Terror under Robespierre led to widespread executions.
- The Revolution's legacy remains debated by historians, balancing its democratic ideals against the violence used to achieve them.
